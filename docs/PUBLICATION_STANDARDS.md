## Natural academic writing

Write in a clear, technically precise voice, with concrete claims and evidence. The researchers should understand the reasoning and be able to defend it. Use their own notes, explanations and revisions to develop that voice; never invent an author's experience or understanding.

During revision, check for generic openings, stock transitions, inflated importance, redundant conclusions, repetitive paragraph templates, unnecessary synonyms and sentences that sound impressive but say little. Let sentence and paragraph length follow the argument. Preserve consistent technical terminology, necessary qualifications and meaningful differences between estimation, adaptation, detection and mitigation.

These are editorial judgments, not blanket word bans or arbitrary punctuation quotas. Do not weaken methods or erase uncertainty to make prose sound more confident. Preserve original attribution, citation accuracy and the target venue's required disclosure of AI assistance. No claim of human-only authorship or guaranteed AI-detector outcome is implied by this writing preference.

## Strong, distinctive titles

The user wants titles with the precision and memorability associated with strong research articles. Current project titles are provisional working labels, not final choices.

When the question and contribution are sufficiently clear:

1. Inspect a small set of relevant papers from the intended field/venue to understand how they convey a problem, mechanism or finding. Learn structure rather than borrowing a famous paper's wording or method name.
2. Produce several materially different title candidates: a direct descriptive title, a mechanism-centered title and, if the method genuinely merits a name, a short name with an informative subtitle.
3. Check that every substantive word is supported by the study. Terms such as adaptive, causal, robust, trustworthy, secure or real-time need the corresponding method/evidence and clear scope.
4. Prefer specific, readable, searchable wording. Remove decorative claims such as revolutionary or groundbreaking; avoid forced acronyms and long lists of technologies.
5. Search for confusing title/method-name collisions and verify the chosen venue's current title rules.
6. Let the researcher choose; revisit the title after results and limitations are known. A negative or narrower finding may warrant a different title.

Do not optimize titles for fame, citations or immigration language at the expense of scientific accuracy. Memorable wording should make the contribution easier to understand.
